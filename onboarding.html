Context & Problem
Onboarding that opens the door,
but never shows the house.

[Borrador] Today's onboarding and customization experience is effectively null. Users move through several steps — sign up, permissions, legal consent — before the app shows anything that resembles what makes TheFork useful for them. None of those steps communicate the value proposition, so people reach the home screen without understanding why location, food preferences, or a shortlist even matter.

Steps without a "why"
[Borrador] Sign-up, location and legal screens are shown back-to-back with no framing — users can't tell how each step will shape what they see next.
Value prop arrives too late
[Borrador] Personalization only becomes visible deep inside the app, once the user is already browsing — by then the onboarding has already lost their attention.
Low engagement, low completion
[Borrador] A generic, undifferentiated flow gives no reason to finish it — drop-off is highest exactly where personalization should start paying off.
Skip is the path of least resistance
[Borrador] Every preference step can be skipped, and with nothing shown in return for answering, most users take the exit.
The Solution · Value Prop in 3 Steps
Show the value before asking for anything back.

[Borrador] The proposal collapses onboarding into three understandable, sequential moments — each one earning the next. We tell the user why we need their location, learn what they like and dislike, and reward that input immediately with a handpicked list of restaurants before they ever reach the generic home screen.

Step 1 · Location
Where would you like to eat?
[Borrador] A single, low-friction choice — type a city or share location — framed around a clear benefit: finding the best spots nearby, not filling out a form.
Step 2 · Taste
Which types of food do you want to try?
[Borrador] Removable cuisine chips instead of a dropdown — quick to scan, quick to adjust, and framed as recommendations rather than a filter form.
Step 3 · Payoff
TheFork Shortlist for you!
[Borrador] Before landing on the generic home screen, the user sees a small, ranked set of restaurants that already reflect their answers — proof the previous two steps were worth it.
The Flow
From splash to a personalized first screen

[Borrador] Twelve screens, three decisions. Sign-up and legal consent stay where they need to for compliance, but every preference-gathering step is paired with an immediate, visible payoff.
