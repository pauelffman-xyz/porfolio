The mandate
I wasn't handed a brief — I was asked to take the lead on the home.

61K users, 300K orders, 1,268 vendors — and a home everyone agreed was underperforming without agreeing on why. Open scope meant I owned the framing: research first, then two directions, then a defended decision. The output of this project isn't a screen. It's the argument that produced it.

Outdated & unattractive UI
Disorganized layout. Icons don't match their categories. A negative first impression that costs trust before the first tap.
Discoverability failure
Users can't quickly find products. Too much time searching → frustration → abandonment.
No reorder or personalization
Can't find favourite stores. Can't repeat the last purchase. The app feels generic to a user who orders the same lunch every week.
Content blind to context
The same promos at 8am and 8pm, for every user. Merchandising that ignores time, preference and location.
Research · Discovery
No brief. No backlog ticket.
Just: "take the lead on this."

I was brought in without a defined scope — the home "worked", but nobody could explain why it wasn't converting. So instead of redesigning a screen, I started by defining the question the home actually had to answer: is this a place to show what monchis is, or a place to get to the next order fast? Everything that follows — including the two paths — comes from that tension.

Method 01
Behavioural data
Amplitude across 61K users and 300K orders. Where taps land, which verticals get entered, where sessions die.
Method 02
Active listening
User feedback and support inputs, coded into themes: discoverability, speed, personalization, promo visibility.
Method 03
Heuristic audit
Mapping what the old home declared as hierarchy vs. what it actually delivered above the fold.
Method 04
Marketplace benchmark
Regional delivery and marketplace patterns for header, search persistence and category legibility.

Findings · What the research forced
Five findings.
Five non-negotiables.

I only kept findings that changed a decision. Each one is paired with the constraint it imposed on any design direction — which is exactly what made two paths possible instead of one.

01
1 / 5
Search was already the front door — dressed as a form field
60% of users engage with search, yet it sat as a static input with a generic placeholder, scrolling out of view the moment anyone explored.
Constraint
Search must be permanently reachable, and it has to say something.
02
Users arrive with intent, not curiosity
80% go straight into Restaurants; burgers and pizza alone carry 67%+ of category traffic. The home was optimising for discovery for people who had already decided.
Constraint
Everything actionable has to fit above the fold. Brand cannot own the first screen.
03
The product didn't know what time it was
Beer promos at 8am. The same content for every user, every hour. Users read this as the app not knowing them — a trust problem, not a merchandising one.
Constraint
Home content must be time- and preference-aware, or not shown at all.
04
Zero memory between sessions
No favourites, no reorder, no trace of the last purchase — in a product where the same lunch repeats week after week.
Constraint
The home needs a personalization slot high in the page, not a "recommended" afterthought.
05
Categories had to be decoded, not read
Icons didn't match their verticals and unlabeled imagery forced interpretation — a bag of groceries could plausibly be Mercado or Comidas.
Constraint
If it's a vertical, it carries a label. No guessing.
Wireframes · Exploration
Structure first,
pixels much later.

Low fidelity was where the real arguments got settled: block order, how many verticals survive above the fold, where the personalization slot lives, and what happens to the header on scroll. It's also where the sharpest idea of the project appeared — a contextual box driven by the moment of the day — and where I worked out the two things that make an idea like that survivable: how much space it's allowed to take from commercial content, and what happens when the user dismisses it. Carrying those unresolved trade-offs into review is what made two complete paths necessary instead of one polished opinion.

The exploration · Time-of-day box
Where the box lives — after the user, before the catalogue
Greeting and favourites open the page, the offer banner and category row follow, and only then the contextual box: "¿Qué te tienta esta mañana?" with two stacked rails — an intent shortcut ("¿Fan de los desayunos?" · medialunas, café, tostados) and "Más favoriteados a esta hora". Commercial sections keep running underneath.
Rule
The box is dismissible — and if it's closed, it returns the next day with a different moment. Personalization that can't be refused stops being a service and becomes a banner.
Same component, four moments
Morning, midday, afternoon and night are not four sections — they're one component with different content and copy. "¿Qué te tienta esta mañana?" at 8:40 becomes "¿No almorzaste aún?" at 12:30, "Merienda con algo dulce" at 17:10, "Cena sin vueltas" at 21:00.

Rules
Time of day × order history × stock and delivery time. When there's no signal strong enough to personalise, it falls back to "más pedidos cerca tuyo" — the component never shows an empty or generic state pretending to be personal.
Why this mattered

Defining one component with four content states — instead of four hardcoded sections — is what made time-awareness affordable to build and to maintain. The rules and the fallback were written at wireframe stage, before a single visual decision, which is why the feature survived scoping.

One context engine, two surfaces
Intent chips move up next to the search bar — desayuno · café · mercado — and opening search reveals a sheet built from the same signals as the box: "A esta hora se pide" (café con medialunas, desayuno saludable, tostado + jugo), then last orders, then what's trending nearby.
Key
The sheet suggestions are the box suggestions — one engine, rendered twice. That's what turned the animated placeholder from a copy trick into a system, and it's the direct ancestor of the dynamic search that shipped.

Wireframe · Banner logic
Three banner types, none of them generic.

Top locals running a promo, a match against onboarding preferences, and a payment incentive. Defining the three cases at wireframe stage is what kept the slot from turning into undifferentiated ad space once real content arrived.

The two paths · V1 vs V2
Two complete directions,
not one safe proposal.

The findings pointed clearly at what the home had to do — but not at how much brand we were willing to trade for speed. Rather than pick for the business, I built both answers to the same brief, each internally consistent down to error and loading states. It moved the review from "I like this one" to "which cost are we choosing".

Path V1 · Brand-led
The home as
a statement.
An extended red header owns the entry: saved address, greeting and search all live inside the brand block. Promo carousel takes the hero slot, verticals follow as an image mosaic.
Where it wins
✓ Address is impossible to miss — lowest risk of ordering to the wrong place
✓ The proximity alert ("estás lejos de tu casa") has a natural home inside the header
✓ Strongest brand recall on first frame
Where it costs
✕ The red block eats the first screen — verticals get pushed below the fold
✕ Search inherits the visual weight of the header and competes with the promo
✕ Unlabeled category mosaic leaves finding F05 unresolved
Path V2 · Task-led
The home as
a shortcut.
Neutral surface. Search is the first thing on screen, address one level below it. Labeled vertical cards move up; the promo becomes a module instead of the doorway.
Where it wins
✓ Search, address and all five verticals fit above the fold
✓ Photo + label kills the guessing game — Comidas, Mercado, Tiendas, Pick up, ueno
✓ Scales: a sixth vertical doesn't break the layout
Where it costs
✕ Much quieter brand presence on the first frame
✕ Promo loses the hero slot — needed a separate conversation with commercial
✕ The proximity alert loses its container and has to be re-solved
V1 · Contextual alert & states
The red header earned its space by preventing an expensive error.

Ordering to the wrong address costs money, time and trust — so in V1 location isn't just displayed, it warns: "¡Cuidado! Estás lejos de tu casa. ¿Querés hacer el pedido igual?", dismissible and never blocking. The same pass covered the states nobody designs: skeleton loading on the real grid, and a persistent offline snackbar.

· Proximity alert docked inside the header block
· Dismissible with an explicit "order anyway" path
· Skeleton mirrors the final layout — no content jump
· Offline snackbar over the header, never blocking nav
V2 · Scroll behaviour & states
The header had to survive the scroll.

Putting search at the top only works if it stays there. In V2 the header collapses into a compact sticky bar — search plus abbreviated address — and a floating "Volver arriba" returns the full context without manual scrolling. What V2 had to solve separately was the alert: without a red block to dock into, the proximity warning becomes a contextual snackbar.

· Collapsed sticky header — search never leaves the screen
· Back-to-top after deep scroll
· Address abbreviated, never dropped
· Same skeleton and offline rules as V1 — states are shared, not per-path

The call
V2 won on four of the five findings. V1 won on the one that costs real money.
So the final home isn't V2 — it's V2's spine with V1's safeguards grafted on. Task-first structure, labeled verticals and the sticky search from V2; the contextual proximity alert and the address prominence from V1, re-expressed as a contextual snackbar instead of a permanent red block. The promo kept its reach by moving from doorway to module — which is the trade the commercial team ultimately signed off on.
The final version
What actually
shipped.

V2's structure survived contact with delivery, and the personalization layer the wireframes had been arguing for finally landed on top of it: favourite places, time-aware carousels, and a search bar that stopped being a field and started being a conversation.

Task-first spine — from V2
Search on top, labeled verticals above the fold, promo as a module. The sticky collapsing header carries search through the whole page.
Contextual safeguards — from V1
Proximity alert and address confirmation preserved, re-expressed as contextual snackbars rather than a permanent brand block.
The personalization layer — new
Favourite places, reorder and time-aware sections. This is the piece that later produced the highest CVR of any carousel on the page.
Final · V2 spine + V1 safeguards
Feature Focus · Animated Dynamic Search

Both paths agreed on one thing and disagreed on everything else — search had to lead. V2 won the structure argument, but this is the component that made the decision worth making: once search is permanently on screen, it stops being a field you fill and becomes the surface that carries context.

The search
that thinks.

The animated search bar is the emotional centerpiece of the new home. Instead of a static placeholder, it rotates contextual suggestions based on time of day, user history, and active promotions — making the app feel alive, personal, and aware.

Preference-matched — reflects onboarding choices: "Mirá el sushi con delivery gratis"
Promotion-aware — surfaces active deals: "Hamburguesas al 50% OFF hoy"

Key Design Decisions
Decision 01
Two paths instead of one proposal
V1 brand-led vs V2 task-led, both built to the same findings. It turned the review from a taste debate into an explicit trade-off — and the final home borrows from both.
Decision 02
Animated dynamic search
Static placeholder → contextual rotating text. Time, user prefs, promotions. 60% click rate validates the investment.
Decision 03
Time-aware carousels
Beer promos at 8am told users the app didn't know them. Content that adapts to context = trust.
Decision 04
Favorite places as hero feature
44.6% CVR. The highest performing carousel by far — personalization beats any other content strategy.
Decision 05
Personalized promo banners
3 types: Top locals with promo, onboarding match, payment incentive. Contextual, never generic.
Decision 06
Rapid execution constraint
Prioritized aesthetic improvements over new features. Visual facelift first — data then drives feature investment.
Decision 07
Reusable component system
Documentation reduced QA cycles and enabled team to move faster on next iterations. System = product investment.
Learning 01
Two directions beat one recommendation
Brought V1 and V2 fully resolved, states included — turned opinion into an explicit trade-off.
Learning 02
Personalization is the killer feature
44.6% CVR came from customization, not visual polish — users convert when the product understands them.
Learning 03
Context-awareness is trust
Time-aware carousels weren't aesthetic — they were a trust decision that changed the emotional relationship with the product.
Learning 04
Documentation ships features
A real design system reduced QA cycles and enabled faster iteration — system thinking is a product investment.
The result
"Personalization is the killer feature."
The 44.6% CVR came from building a product that feels like it knows the user — every "obvious" decision had a measurable counterpart in the data.
